# CLAUDE.md — english

Domain-specific instructions. Loaded after the root `CLAUDE.md` at the start of every
session in this domain. Keep it short and factual.

## Goal

Take the learner from a rusty school-level base to **building their own everyday
sentences** and **reading ordinary, non-technical text** (messages, simple articles,
descriptions) with comfort. The target is functional everyday English — not academic
mastery, not exam prep. Confidence to compose and to read, not to pass IELTS.

## Starting level

Adult, strong programmer. Recognizes a lot of English **passively** from exposure
(UI labels, GitHub project context, common tech words) — can guess meaning by
"насмотренность". But **active** English is weak: roughly leftover middle-school level.
Cannot reliably build a correct sentence from scratch, shaky on tenses, articles,
word order, and everyday (non-tech) vocabulary.

Freeze this — progress goes into `memory.md`, not here.

## Methodology

- **Build, don't just recognize.** The whole point is to move skills from passive to
  active. Every theory block ends in the learner *producing* English, not just reading it.
- **Word order first.** English is rigid SVO; Russian is free. This single difference
  causes most beginner errors, so it is the backbone of the early modules.
- **Anchor on Russian contrasts.** Constantly compare to Russian: no cases, fixed word
  order, mandatory subject, articles (which Russian lacks), the `to be` that Russian
  drops. Name the trap a Russian speaker walks into, every time.
- **High-frequency vocabulary.** Teach the ~800–1000 words that cover most everyday
  text first; skip rare/technical words. Vocabulary rides inside example sentences, not
  as bare lists.
- **Everyday register, not technical.** Examples are about coffee, weather, friends,
  shops, plans — deliberately *not* about code. The learner already absorbs tech English
  by osmosis; this domain fills the бытовой gap.
- **Drill over passive reading.** Each module ends with `drill` blocks; SRS on `cards`.

## Preferred sources / authors

Real, verifiable references only:
- **Raymond Murphy — *English Grammar in Use*** (Cambridge) — the canonical
  self-study grammar, unit-per-topic. The spine of the grammar track.
- **Oxford Learner's Dictionaries** — oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com — meanings,
  pronunciation (IPA + audio), example sentences.
- **Cambridge Dictionary** — dictionary.cambridge.org — RU glosses + IPA.
- **The Oxford 3000 / 5000** — frequency-ranked core word lists, for vocabulary priority.

## Explanation style

- Content in **Russian**; all English words/sentences stay in English. Give the Russian
  gloss right after, and for the first appearance of a word show **IPA** in brackets
  (e.g. `weather [ˈweðə(r)]`).
- **Engaging, not dry.** Write so the learner *wants* to keep going: conversational
  tone, vivid everyday scenes, light humor where it lands. The joke serves the point.
- Lead with intuition ("почему так"), then the rule. A short mental model beats a table.
- Show the **literal word-for-word breakdown** when contrasting with Russian word order
  (e.g. `I am a student` → дословно «Я есть один студент»).
- Show the wrong sentence a Russian speaker would build, then the fix — learning from
  the error.
- Use natural, current British/American English; note big differences when they matter,
  don't drown the learner in dialect trivia.

## Out of scope

- **Technical/programming English** — the learner already gets this by exposure.
- **Exam prep** (IELTS/TOEFL formats, scoring tricks).
- **Heavy phonetics drilling / accent reduction** — give IPA and clear pronunciation
  notes, but pronunciation perfection is not the goal.
- **Business/formal correspondence** register — focus is everyday speech and reading.
